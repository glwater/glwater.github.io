The state of Texas has 2,770 districts, with the Greater Houston area hosting 1,416 of these (slightly over 50% of the districts in the state). Houston-based law firm [Schwartz, Page & Harding](https://www.sphllp.com/) administers [203 districts](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1soVNlqDWHr-RBFzjNem_CSAANjOC48Y9/view) in the Greater Houston area—14-15% of the districts in the Greater Houston area and 7-8% of the districts in the state. This is so much control of administrative, legal, and even regulatory power in one law firm. How is this firm monitored and audited for compliance with its legal and fiduciary duties? When they breach the law, who is holding them accountable and how?

This raises a central issue of concentration of influence, accountability, transparency, and conflicts of interest in the governance of public water districts. Important public-governance concerns become: 

When one private law firm provides legal and administrative services to a substantial percentage of public water districts, what independent safeguards ensure that those districts are being governed in the public interest rather than becoming dependent on a concentrated network of private professional service providers?  <br><br>
Who actually oversees the law firm and is the oversight genuine, meaningful, and effective? [TCEQ](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/), the auditor, [State Bar of Texas](https://www.texasbar.com/), and the Board of Directors. Unfortunately, persistent, long-lasting real-life incidents show that none of the four parties is doing its job.
{: .flash2}

What are the structural risks of concentrated professional influence❓ Too many:
- (Destructive) Regulatory influence.
- Institutional dependency.
- Conflicts of interest and overlapping representation.
- Reduced competition and barriers to entry.
- Concentration of specialized knowledge.
- Financial concentration and public debt.
- Close relationships and the revolving-door problem.
- Homogenization of legal advice and the risk of wrong legal interpretations.
- Reduced transparency.
- Limited consumer choice and unequal access to influence.
- Taxpayer vulnerability.
- Weak independent review.
- The accountability gap.
- Systemic risk.

Is the existing oversight structure sufficient❓ Absolutely not. 

📌 In calendar year 2026, our Grand Lakes MUD/WCID attorneys ([Christopher Todd Skinner](https://www.sphllp.com/_chris_skinner.html) and [Gordon Christopher Cranner](https://www.sphllp.com/_gordon_cranner.html) of [Schwartz, Page & Harding](https://www.sphllp.com/)) are on track to close the year charging [Grand Lakes MUD No. 2](https://www.grandlakesmud2.com/) some **$400,000** in legal fees, on top of **$216,365** paid to [Lloyd Gosselink Rochelle & Townsend](https://www.lglawfirm.com/). The two attorneys defiantly brag about—they claim—citizens' unsuccessful efforts to bring them to justice despite complaints to the [TCEQ](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/), [Texas Attorney General](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/), [FBI Houston](https://www.fbi.gov/contact-us/field-offices/houston), and [State Bar of Texas](https://www.texasbar.com/). Who holds professional advisors accountable when their actions contribute to violations of law or breaches of fiduciary obligations? What protections exist for residents and taxpayers who believe their district is not being properly governed?

📌 Public accountability cannot depend exclusively on the judgment of the same professional network that advises the entities being monitored. When private professional interests and public responsibilities intersect across hundreds of governmental entities, who independently ensures that the public interest comes first?

📌 It is very naïve to think that a law firm is compliant and doing its legal, fiduciary, ethical, moral, and civic duties just because it has served a water district for 40 years. The existing system lacks sufficient safeguards to prevent conflicts, institutional dependency, anticompetitive practices, and failures of accountability. Legal advisors, financial advisors, directors, and other professional service providers must remain accountable to the public purpose established by the Legislature, but they are not. They currently operate in a regulatory vacuum, where the systemic absence of state oversight has fostered an environment of unchecked autonomy, defiance of the spirit of public service, prioritization of self-preservation over the public interest, and entire insulation from meaningful consequences.

📌 When the law firms hired by public water districts (such as MUDs and WCIDs) help draft the very legislation and [TCEQ](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/) rules that govern them, the result is a fictional system of oversight. This creates a conflict of interest where these firms essentially police themselves, routinely clearing their own practices of wrongdoing. However, their actual conduct, administrative fees, and regulatory compliance tell a completely different story.

📌 Urgent, meaningful structural safeguards and reforms need to be put in place to protect district and taxpayer interests, improve transparency, identify unlawful patterns across districts, periodically evaluate district consultants and boards, reduce institutional dependency, strengthen public participation, give residents a meaningful oversight role, enforce accountability, and eliminate impunity.
