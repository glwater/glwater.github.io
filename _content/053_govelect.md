Our Portal sent a formal Call to Action to [Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa](https://ginafortexas.com/) urging the incoming administration and the 90th Texas Legislature to end vendor monopolies, enforce open-government laws, and put water districts back under public accountability. Governor-elect Hinojosa,  

### &nbsp; Urgent Reform Needed: Concentration of Legal and Administrative Influence Over Texas Water Districts: Who Is Watching the Watchdogs?  

Texas has established thousands of special-purpose water districts to provide essential public infrastructure and services. These districts exercise governmental authority, incur public debt, levy taxes, and make decisions that directly affect property owners and taxpayers. Yet a significant portion of these public entities rely on a relatively small number of private professional-service providers.  

[Schwartz, Page & Harding](https://www.sphllp.com/) serves 203 districts in the Greater Houston area, representing roughly 14.3% of the region's 1,416 districts and 7.3% of the state's 2,770 districts. These figures raise an important public-policy question: Has the concentration of specialized legal and administrative services created an imbalance of institutional influence over Texas water district governance?  

The issue is not simply how many clients a law firm represents. It is the potential accumulation of institutional knowledge, regulatory influence, professional relationships, conflicts of interest, and recurring financial engagements across hundreds of separate public entities.  

When the same professional network participates in district formation, legal governance, bond financing, infrastructure contracts, and regulatory matters, what independent safeguards ensure that the interests of individual districts and their taxpayers remain protected? Is the existing oversight structure sufficient to prevent conflicts, institutional dependency, anticompetitive practices, and failures of accountability?  

District financial audits are not the same as independent audits of legal compliance, professional conduct, potential conflicts of interest, or the quality and independence of legal advice.  

The public deserves answers to several fundamental questions:  
- Who independently evaluates the legal advice provided to these public entities?  
- Who monitors potential conflicts of interest arising from overlapping professional relationships?  
- Who verifies that legal and financial services are competitively procured?  
- Who investigates recurring patterns of questionable practices?  
- Who holds professional advisors accountable when their actions contribute to violations of law or breaches of fiduciary obligations?  
- What protections exist for residents and taxpayers whose districts are not being properly governed?  

Public accountability cannot depend exclusively on the judgment of the same professional network that advises the entities being monitored. The concentration of professional influence deserves the same level of scrutiny that we would apply to concentration of ownership, voting power, or financial influence in a large corporation.  

Water districts exist to serve the public. Their legal advisors, financial advisors, directors, and other professional service providers must remain accountable to that public purpose.  

The fundamental question is simple: When private professional interests and public responsibilities intersect across hundreds of governmental entities, who independently ensures that the public interest comes first?  

### &nbsp; A Call to Action for the Incoming Administration and the 90th Texas Legislature
Texas water-district governance requires urgent, decisive intervention from the Governor’s Office and the Legislature. Across thousands of districts, public oversight has eroded into an entrenched, self-policing network that operates with little fear of consequences.  

For decades, the same closed circle of private vendors—legal counsel, operators, engineers, financial advisors, bookkeepers, auditors, tax collectors, delinquent-tax attorneys, trash contractors, and web-hosting providers—has remained embedded in the same districts year after year. Insulated by weak oversight, these firms routinely withhold critical records from residents, reducing open government, transparency, and fiduciary accountability to empty slogans.  

Worse still, this culture of impunity has become brazen. In our own community, district legal counsel has gone so far as to boast in writing that taxpayers have failed to hold them accountable despite 18 months of formal complaints filed with state regulators and law enforcement authorities.

As you prepare to take office in January 2027, we urge you and the Legislature to make water-district reform a priority:  

- Establish independent oversight of district legal counsel, financial advisors, and long-term contractors.  
- Enforce strict transparency and Public Information Act compliance with real penalties for withholding public records.  
- Mandate competitive procurement and term-review requirements to break up decades-old vendor monopolies.  
- Empower state agencies and the Public Utility Commission to investigate systemic conflicts of interest and protect ratepayers from unchecked legal and administrative billing.  

Texans deserve water districts that answer to the voters and taxpayers who fund them—not to an unaccountable circle of private contractors.  

On behalf of Grand Lakes:  
A Community Asking Who Is Accountable for Its Water Districts.
