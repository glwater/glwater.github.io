The [Forvis Mazars](https://www.forvismazars.us/) FY2025 audit report for [Grand Lakes MUD No. 2](https://www.grandlakesmud2.com/) identified material weaknesses in internal control. Among other things, the audit identified weaknesses concerning the preparation and review of financial statements and capital-asset and depreciation information. The auditor stated that management lacked sufficient ability to prevent, detect, and correct potential material misstatements.

The district's response essentially argued that the arrangement was customary for small Texas special districts and that hiring additional personnel or consultants to strengthen controls would not be cost-effective. That explanation deserves careful public examination. The numbers we presented in the [previous article](./018_scale-money) tell a different story. Additionally, the issue is not whether a small governmental entity may use outside professionals. The issue is whether the entity has meaningful independent controls over those professionals.

A consultant preparing financial information should not be the only person capable of understanding that information. A consultant managing contracts should not be the only person evaluating those contracts. A consultant advising the board should not effectively control what the board sees. The Grand Lakes water districts have no conventional employee structure. Instead, they depend extensively on outside consultants for legal, engineering, operational, bookkeeping, financial, and other functions. This arrangement makes effective board oversight more important—not less.

### The MUD Board's Treatment of the FY2025 Audit Report
In the December 15, 2025, MUD2 meeting, the board was briefed on the FY2025 [audit report](https://www.grandlakesmud2.com/static/aca54eeea16d936f13749fa12691dea7/Audit_FYE_2025_Final_Audit_Report_GLMUD_2_1_a83e444f68.pdf) (*see* page 2/7 of the [minutes](https://www.grandlakesmud2.com/static/8488320762686cfd23a3cccb3269d845/12_15_2025_Approved_Minutes_GL_MUD_2_1_59656739ee.pdf), which reads in part):
>
<u>AUDIT REPORT</u> Ms. Ramirez then presented to and reviewed with the Board a draft of the District's Independent Auditor's Report and Financial Statements for the fiscal year ended August 31, 2025 (the "Audit Report"), a copy of which is attached hereto as **Exhibit B**. Mr. \[Christopher Todd\] Skinner \[MUD attorney and part of Schwartz, Page & Harding\] advised the Board that SPH has not yet completed its review of the draft Audit Report, and recommended its approval, subject to SPH's final review and approval. After discussion, Director Henry moved that: (i) the Audit Report for fiscal year ended August 31, 2025, be approved, subject to final review and approval by SPH; (ii) the response to the associated Management Letter be approved; (iii) the President be authorized to execute the required Annual Filing Affidavit on behalf of the Board and the District; and (iv) such Audit Report and Annual Filing Affidavit be filed with the appropriate governmental authorities, including the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (the "TCEQ") and the Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, by the applicable deadline. Director Baker seconded said motion, which unanimously carried.

### Core Principles of MUD Outsourcing and Fiduciary Duty
An elected Board of Directors for a Texas Municipal Utility District (MUD) holds non-delegable public trust and governance responsibilities. While MUDs routinely hire outside consultants—such as general counsel, engineers, operators, bookkeepers, auditors, and financial advisors—contracting those services out does not legally or ethically relieve the elected directors of their fiduciary duty to the district and its taxpayers.

- **Public Office Standard:** MUD directors act as public officials managing tax-backed public funds and vital infrastructure (water, sewer, and drainage); their conscience and legal loyalty must remain with the residents of the district, not the vendors they hire.
- **Selection and Monitoring Duty:** Under standard governance and public law frameworks, a board has an affirmative obligation to prudently vet, hire, supervise, and regularly evaluate third-party consultants.
- **Duty of Care:** Board members are legally required to be fully informed before making decisions. Conditional approvals stretch this principle, as directors are placing blind trust in a third party to finalize public financial records.
- **The Fiduciary Concern:** Voting to approve a financial document and an Annual Filing Affidavit before a professional firm has finalized its review means the board is technically signing a blank check—approving an incomplete or unverified product. If that final review requested any changes, the board would have effectively authorized a document they never actually saw in its final form, thus introducing a significant governance risk.
- **Delegation vs. Abdication:** Directors may delegate technical, operational, and legal tasks to specialized professionals, but they cannot legally surrender final decision-making authority, policy judgment, or accountability.
- **Delegation Limits:** While a board can delegate the preparation of an audit to an independent auditor and the review to legal counsel, the ultimate statutory liability for the accuracy of those financial disclosures rests squarely on the elected board members.
- **Retained Liability:** Relying blindly on an external attorney's or consultant's recommendation does not automatically insulate a board member if the board failed to exercise reasonable care and independent business judgment.

### Remarkable Irregularities Prove the MUD Board's Breach of Their Very Basic Duties
The following irregularities irrefutably prove that in accepting the MUD attorney's request to approve the audit report and sign a representation / management letter and an affidavit for filing with the [TCEQ](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/), when the attorney's review of the audit report was **not** yet completed, the MUD's board abdicated their fundamental duties. Upon a thorough investigation, it could be found that the MUD board members were not only entrapped, but they also contributed to the realization of a major plot.

- Item no. 9, page 2/6, in the December 15, 2025, [representation](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1QfKGZnqlYwSkTeeFl4eXQoi33eJ4V4a1/view) signed by MUD board president, Michael Christopher McClusky, falsely claims the MUD was not involved in litigation during the 2025 fiscal year (September 1, 2024 to August 31, 2025), and did not seek or receive legal services in relation to litigation. While he lacks any power or authority to institute suits on behalf of the MUD, MUD attorney [Christopher Todd Skinner](https://www.sphllp.com/_chris_skinner) of [Schwartz, Page & Harding](https://www.sphllp.com/) instructed Austin-based law firm of [Lloyd Gosselink Rochelle & Townsend](https://www.lglawfirm.com/) to file the suit [D-1-GN-24-010136](https://odysseyweb.traviscountytx.gov/app/RegisterOfActions/#/565B23C172A97821F23F1F46A3882F80DDE8728AEBE9CFD1EF96638D80E0350690EA0B93D4AE3EF05BDDB8D56BE52A9B523EA841C4A996602187153905F1FC896E8334F35A5A7584DD78B722E5373307) before the Travis County 345th District Court against the Texas Office of the Attorney General ([OAG](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/)). They did so on December 20, 2024. Continuing to be active per the present, the suit intends to block the release of legal invoices the OAG declared public in its opinion [OR-2024-040867](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1s8t0KcQdvODIKJLJdhvNqW4e9VAF59dj/view) of December 6, 2024.

- For brevity and focus, we will limit the discussion to the suit D-1-GN-24-010136, while noting that the MUD was—and continues to be—involved in a number of other suits; something that could not be but completely obvious and beyond doubt to the MUD board and MUD attorney.

- McClusky, who served as MUD board president since 2002, could not be naïve, confused, or ignorant. He cannot be excused for signing a representation that he did not read. McClusky could have been entrapped by the MUD attorney, Skinner, but—equally—he could have signed, well knowing what he was doing. 

- It is visibly noticed that McClusky's signatures on the [representation](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1QfKGZnqlYwSkTeeFl4eXQoi33eJ4V4a1/view) and [affidavit](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1fElEgoIBJOTW31ojDxKQrIHWB94m3_wL/view) differ from his signature on his Texas Ethics Commission [Campaign Finance Filing](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Uw1k64wrHBY1BB5ISvLbZyuboC6cktJd/view). Signature forgery and tampering with governmental records are criminal offenses under Texas Penal Code §§ [32.21](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=PE&chapter=PE.32&artSec=32.21) and [37.10](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=PE&chapter=PE.37&artSec=37.10). Did the [Schwartz, Page & Harding](https://www.sphllp.com/) staff plan and execute a plot? That is left to the authorities to investigate and determine.

- Melia Anne Berry, a [Schwartz, Page & Harding](https://www.sphllp.com/) paralegal since 2005, [surrendered her notary commission](/assets/docs/20260522_Berry-NCsurr.pdf) to the Texas Secretary of State on May 22, 2026. While the surrender relates to different circumstances, it still indicates that the paralegal is at ease with irregularities. And this gains extra weight given that she administered the December 15, 2025, affidavit, where McClusky's signature looks suspicious. 

### Conclusions
📌 It is a clear illustration of governance breakdown when public officials charged with managing tax-backed funds rely blindly on outside vendors without robust, independent verification. By rubber-stamping financial disclosures and legal representations that contain verifiable falsehoods, the board did not merely slip into a passive oversight role—they effectively handed the keys of the district over to the very consultants they were elected to supervise. Whether this pattern of behavior stems from systemic entrapment by entrenched specialized professionals or an intentional sidestepping of statutory visibility, the consequences for the public trust remain identical. When an elected body abdicates its core analytical duties, transparency is suppressed, financial risk multiplies, and the line between public service and private vendor protection completely disappears.
