    Guide: The Texas Public Information Act in Brief


    The Texas Public Information Act (PIA) is one of the principal laws through which Texans exercise their right to know what their government is doing. Its philosophy is direct. Texas law states that the people are entitled, unless the law expressly provides otherwise, to complete information about the affairs of government and the official acts of public officials and employees. The law also says that it should be liberally construed in favor of granting a request for information.

    Texas Government Code § 552.001. Policy; Construction.
    (a) Under the fundamental philosophy of the American constitutional form of representative government that adheres to the principle that government is the servant and not the master of the people, it is the policy of this state that each person is entitled, unless otherwise expressly provided by law, at all times to complete information about the affairs of government and the official acts of public officials and employees. The people, in delegating authority, do not give their public servants the right to decide what is good for the people to know and what is not good for them to know. The people insist on remaining informed so that they may retain control over the instruments they have created. The provisions of this chapter shall be liberally construed to implement this policy.

    What is public information?

    The definition is broad. Public information generally includes information that is written, produced, collected, assembled, or maintained in connection with official business by a governmental body. It can also include information maintained for a governmental body when the governmental body owns it, has a right of access to it, or spends or contributes public money for its creation or maintenance. This is particularly important for modern water districts because so much district business is performed by private contractors. That, however, does not mean that a private consultant can automatically transform governmental information into private information. If records are created or maintained for a governmental body in connection with its official business, such records become owned by the district and are subject to the PIA.

    The district cannot simply decide what the public may know

    The Texas statute expressly rejects the concept that public officials may decide for themselves what information is “good” or “bad” for the public to know. Instead, disclosure is the general rule, while withholding information requires a legal basis. There are, however, exceptions in the statute. Some information is confidential by law. Other information may be excepted from disclosure under specific statutory provisions. The existence of an exception must therefore be evaluated under the law rather than assumed.

    How does a resident request information?

    A person may submit a request for public information to the governmental body's officer for public information or the appropriate agent. The request should describe the information sought with enough specificity for the governmental body to identify the responsive records. The governmental body generally may not ask why the requestor wants the information. It may seek clarification when a request is unclear and may discuss narrowing a very large request, but the purpose of the request is generally not its business.

    “Promptly” means promptly

    The statute requires public information to be produced promptly for inspection or duplication. “Promptly” means as soon as possible under the circumstances, within a reasonable time, without delay.

    The statute also provides that if the governmental body cannot produce the information within 10 business days, it must certify that fact in writing and establish a reasonable date and time when the information will be available (Government Code § 552.221(d)). The 10-business-day provision should not be misunderstood as an automatic ten-business-day grace period for each and every request. The governing standard is prompt production.

    Contractors and consultants do not automatically escape the Act

    This is particularly important for districts that outsource much or all of their governmental work. A district may have private firms handling:

    • Accounting.
    • Bookkeeping.
    • Legal services.
    • Engineering.
    • Operations.
    • Tax collection.
    • Delinquent-tax matters.
    • Financial advising.
    • Communications.
    • Websites.
    • Records.
    • Document management.

    Records relating to the district's official business remain subject to the Public Information Act even when they are physically maintained by a contractor. Accordingly, a serious public-information inquiry may need to include not merely the district's own files, but records maintained for the district by its agents or contractors.

    Email and electronic records matter

    Modern government does not exist only on paper. Relevant public information can exist in: different forms and formats as shown below. The medium does not necessarily determine whether information is public.

    • Email.
    • Electronic documents.
    • Spreadsheets.
    • Databases.
    • Invoices.
    • Contracts.
    • Text or other electronic communications when otherwise subject to the Act.
    • Audio and video.
    • Photographs.
    • Electronically stored financial information.
    • Website records.
    • Electronic correspondence with consultants.

    A district must have a legal basis to withhold information

    The PIA contains numerous exceptions. Examples include attorney-client privileged information, certain personal information, proprietary information, certain law-enforcement information, and other categories specifically protected by law. But “confidential,” “privileged,” “proprietary,” or “attorney-client” should not become generic labels applied to entire collections of documents. The particular statutory exception must fit the particular information. When a governmental body seeks an Attorney General ruling concerning information that it wishes to withhold, the Act establishes specific procedures and deadlines.

    Public information is a right, not a favor

    This distinction is fundamental. A resident requesting district records is not asking the district to do a favor. The resident is exercising a statutory right. Likewise, a district's response should not depend on whether the requestor is friendly, critical, persistent, a homeowner, a taxpayer, a journalist, or a member of an advocacy group. Texas law requires requests to be treated uniformly without regard to the requestor's position, occupation, or status.

    Why the PIA matters to Grand Lakes

    For residents trying to understand decades of district activity, the Public Information Act can be enormously important. Records can help reconstruct:

    • Contracts.
    • Invoices.
    • Payments.
    • Board decisions.
    • Consultant relationships.
    • Engineering projects.
    • Financial transactions.
    • Audits.
    • Budgets.
    • Tax matters.
    • Legal matters.
    • Correspondence.
    • Complaints.
    • Regulatory communications.
    • The history of decisions affecting residents.

    The most powerful question is often not: “What do we think happened?” It is: “What do the district's own records show happened?” That is precisely the type of question for which the Texas Public Information Act exists.
