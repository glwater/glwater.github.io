A **Water Control and Improvement District**, commonly called a **WCID**, is a governmental entity created under Texas law to perform specified water, wastewater, drainage, conservation, reclamation, and related public functions within a defined geographic area.

Like a Municipal Utility District, a WCID is **not a private corporation**. It is a political subdivision of the State of Texas. The principal general-law provisions governing traditional WCIDs are found in [Chapter 51](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.51&artSec=) of the Texas Water Code, together with the provisions of [Chapter 49](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.49&artSec=) that generally apply to water districts and any special legislation applicable to the particular district.

### What does “water control and improvement” mean?
The name reflects the historical purpose of these districts. A WCID may have authority, depending on its particular statutory authority, to develop, acquire, construct, improve, operate, and maintain facilities relating to such matters as below. But the exact powers of a particular WCID cannot be determined merely from its name. Its governing statute, creation documents, special legislation, elections, orders, and other applicable law must be examined.

- Water supply.
- Water distribution.
- Drainage.
- Flood control.
- Conservation.
- Reclamation.
- Irrigation.
- Related infrastructure and public works.

### Who governs a WCID?
A WCID is governed by a **board of directors**. The directors constitute the governing body of the district and exercise the powers assigned to the district by law. The board may retain professionals and contractors to help carry out the district's functions. But professional assistance does **not** eliminate the board's responsibility to govern. The board must exercise the authority given to it by law and must act within the limits of that authority.

### A WCID is a governmental entity
The governmental character of a WCID has practical consequences for residents.

The district is subject to applicable Texas laws concerning governmental transparency, including the [Open Meetings Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=) and [Public Information Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.552&artSec=). Its governing board is therefore accountable to the public.

The district'd meetings must be conducted in accordance with the [Open Meetings Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=). Minutes or recordings of open meetings must be maintained and are public records. The public has a right to attend and, subject to reasonable rules, record open meetings. The [Public Information Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.552&artSec=) likewise establishes a broad policy favoring public access to governmental information, subject to specific statutory exceptions.

### WCIDs and MUDs can look very similar
To a resident, a WCID and a MUD can appear almost identical. Both may engage in the service we list below. The distinction is primarily one of **statutory authority, historical development, and the specific powers granted to the particular district**. Consequently, one should never assume that because another district does something, a particular WCID automatically has the legal authority to do the same thing.

- Provide water and wastewater services.
- Maintain utility infrastructure.
- Levy or collect taxes where authorized.
- Charge rates and fees.
- Issue debt.
- Retain attorneys and engineers.
- Hire operators and accountants.
- Conduct public board meetings.
- Maintain governmental records.

### The board's role is central
A WCID may employ a collection of professional firms. There may be an attorney, an operator, an engineer, a bookkeeper, an auditor, a financial advisor, a tax collector, a delinquent-tax attorney, contractors for maintenance, technology, websites, communications, and other services. All of those relationships make the board's oversight function more—not less—important. The professionals provide expertise. The board provides governance.

### Why residents should understand the distinction
When residents ask questions about a WCID, they should begin with a fundamental question: **Where does the district get its authority to do what it is doing?** The answer should ultimately be found in Texas law and in the legal documents governing the district. A second question follows: **What did the board actually authorize?** And a third: **What evidence demonstrates that the district complied with the law when it acted?**

These questions are particularly important when public money, contracts, taxes, rates, debt, engineering projects, financial statements, audits, or other significant district matters are involved. A WCID is a government created for a public purpose. Its authority is substantial. Its responsibilities are substantial. And the public's right to understand how that authority is exercised is equally important.
