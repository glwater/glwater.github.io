The **[Texas Open Meetings Act (OMA)](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=)**, Texas Government Code [Chapter 551](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=), applies to governmental bodies, including the governing boards of special districts such as MUDs and WCIDs.  It is one of the most important laws protecting the public's ability to see and understand how its governmental bodies conduct public business. The fundamental principle is straightforward: **Governmental deliberation and governmental decision-making generally belong in public, not behind closed doors.** 

### What is a meeting?
The Act is broader than simply the formal monthly meeting printed on a district's calendar. “Deliberation” includes verbal or written exchanges involving a quorum concerning an issue within the governmental body's jurisdiction. A governmental body may therefore not avoid the Act merely by changing the location, format, or informal character of a gathering. The OMA also recognizes various circumstances in which communications or gatherings may or may not constitute a meeting, making the precise facts important.

### Notice is required
A governmental body generally must provide written notice stating, for each meeting, the:

- date
- hour
- place
- subject.

Under the general rule (Government Code [§ 551.043](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=551.043)), notice must be posted in a place readily accessible to the public for at least **three business days** before the scheduled meeting, subject to statutory exceptions. Notice is not simply a procedural formality. The purpose is to give residents a meaningful opportunity to know what their government intends to discuss and, when appropriate, to attend.

### The agenda matters
The subject matter listed in a meeting notice is important. Residents should be able to determine from the posted agenda what governmental business is going to be considered. A board should not use vague agenda language as a substitute for meaningful public notice when the law requires sufficient disclosure of the subject.

### Open meetings are open to the public
The public has a right to attend an open meeting. A governmental body may establish reasonable rules for maintaining order, but it cannot use such rules to destroy the public's right to observe governmental proceedings. Members of the public may also record open meetings, subject to reasonable rules governing the location and manner of recording.

### Minutes or recordings are required
A governmental body must prepare and keep minutes or make a recording of each open meeting. The minutes must state the subject of each deliberation and indicate each vote, order, decision, or other action taken. The minutes and recordings of open meetings are public records. This creates an important accountability mechanism. It is about a principle: **A board's actions should leave a documentary trail.**

### Closed meetings are exceptions—not the rule
Texas law permits closed meetings, commonly called executive or closed sessions, in specifically defined circumstances. Examples include certain matters involving:

- Consultation with attorneys.
- Real property.
- Personnel.
- Certain prospective gifts.
- Certain economic-development negotiations.
- Other matters specifically authorized by statute.

But the existence of an executive-session exception does not mean that a governmental body may simply move any sensitive or controversial subject behind closed doors. The statutory exception must actually apply. And even when a closed meeting is legally permitted, the board must follow the required procedures.

### Final decisions must be made in public
This is especially important. When a governmental body conducts an authorized closed meeting, it generally may deliberate privately within the scope of the applicable exception, but **final action, decision, or vote must be taken in an open meeting**. The public therefore should not be told that an important governmental decision was “made in executive session.” The legal structure generally requires the governmental body to return to open session and take final action publicly.

### Minutes are not optional
The minutes are more than a historical record. They are evidence of what the governmental body did. For residents examining a district's history, minutes can reveal:

- Who attended.
- What was placed on the agenda.
- What was discussed.
- What was authorized.
- How directors voted.
- Which contracts were approved.
- What expenditures were authorized.
- What reports were received.
- What questions were asked.
- What representations were made to the board.

For that reason, minutes should be preserved carefully and reviewed alongside contracts, invoices, financial statements, engineering reports, audits, correspondence, and other records.

### The public has a role
The Open Meetings Act is not merely a law for lawyers and government officials. It is a practical tool for citizens. A resident can:

- Review meeting notices.
- Attend meetings.
- Record open meetings.
- Obtain minutes and recordings.
- Compare agendas with actual actions.
- Compare board decisions with subsequent expenditures.
- Ask whether a decision was actually authorized.
- Examine whether closed-session exceptions were properly used.

The OMA is ultimately about a simple democratic principle: **The public has entrusted government with public power, and the public has a right to see that power exercised lawfully.**
