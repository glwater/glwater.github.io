When residents look at a water district from the outside, the organizational structure can be confusing. There may be a board of directors. There may be an attorney, an operator, an engineer, a bookkeeper, an auditor, a financial advisor, a tax collector, a delinquent-tax attorney, a trash contractor, a website provider. There may be several other companies and professionals involved. With so many people and firms involved, an important question naturally arises: **Who actually controls the district?** The answer is fundamental to understanding accountability.

### The short answer
**The board of directors, not the consultants, is the governing body of the district.** Consultants and contractors may perform important functions. They may possess specialized expertise. They may prepare documents, make recommendations, manage operations, provide legal advice, maintain records, perform accounting work, conduct audits, design infrastructure, collect taxes, or perform other services. But they do **not** thereby become the district's governing body.

> 
The distinction can be summarized very simply and understanding that distinction is essential.
- **The board governs.**  
- Professionals advise and perform.  
- Contractors execute authorized work.  
- **The public has the right to know how the government is being operated.**

### 1. The board: the governing authority
A water district is governed by its board of directors. The board is the governmental body that exercises the district's powers. Among other things, the board may be responsible for decisions concerning:

- Budgets.
- Rates and fees.
- Taxes.
- Contracts.
- Expenditures.
- Debt.
- Construction projects.
- Infrastructure.
- Policies.
- Personnel matters.
- Consultants.
- Operations.
- Financial matters.
- Other district business.

> 
The board may delegate certain functions to employees, agents, operators, or consultants.
- Delegation does **not** mean abdication.
- A director cannot reasonably defend a governmental decision simply by saying: “The consultant told us to do it.”
- The consultant may have provided advice.
- The board made—or should have made—the governmental decision.
- The distinction becomes particularly important when significant public money is involved.

### 2. The attorney: legal advisor, not the government
The counsel for the district could be one of the most influential people in a water district. That influence is frightening and must be managed. Water-district law could be complicated, but it does not mean only an attorney can read it and understand it. There are statutes, regulations, contracts, bond documents, elections, tax laws, open-government requirements, environmental requirements, and other legal considerations. The board therefore needs competent legal advice. But the attorney is not the board. The attorney's role is to advise the district concerning legal matters and represent the district as authorized. The attorney does not ordinarily possess independent authority to:

- Levy a tax.
- Adopt a budget.
- Approve a contract.
- Authorize an expenditure.
- Establish a utility rate.
- Issue district debt.
- Sue a resident or a local business.
- Exercise governmental powers simply because the attorney recommends doing so.

> 
Those are governmental functions belonging to the district and its governing authorities.
- The attorney advises. 
- The board decides.

### 3. The operator: running the system, not running the government
The operator may be involved in some of the most important day-to-day activities of a district. Depending on the contract and the operator's responsibilities, the operator may handle matters involving:

- Water production and distribution.
- Wastewater collection and treatment.
- System maintenance.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Inspections.
- Operational records.
- Emergency response.
- Repairs.
- Reporting.
- Other utility functions.

> 
The operator may have extensive practical control over the physical system.
- Operational responsibility is not the same thing as governmental authority.
- The board remains responsible for the district's governance.
- A useful distinction is: 
  - **The operator operates the system.** 
  - **The board governs the district.**

### 4. The engineer: technical authority, not governmental authority
Engineers may have enormous influence over district expenditures. A major infrastructure project may involve millions of dollars and require years of planning. The expertise an engineer or engineering firm brings is not indispensable; it is available via hundreds of persons or firms that could provide better expertise and more reasonable fees.

The engineer may determine or recommend:
- What needs to be repaired.
- What needs to be replaced.
- What should be constructed.
- What specifications should be used.
- What alternatives are available.
- What a project may cost.
- How a project should be implemented.

> 
Engineering expertise does not itself create governmental authority.
- The engineer should provide professional technical advice.
- The board must determine whether the district should authorize the proposed action, subject to the applicable law and required procedures.
- The difference is important: 
  **“The engineer recommended it”** is **not** the same thing as **“the district authorized it.”**

### 5. The bookkeeper: maintaining financial records, not making financial policy
A bookkeeper may prepare the district's financial records, checks, reconciliations, accounts payable, reports, or other accounting information. That work can be extremely important. But the bookkeeper does not ordinarily determine the district's governmental priorities. The bookkeeper should record transactions accurately and according to the applicable accounting requirements and the district's instructions and authorizations.
