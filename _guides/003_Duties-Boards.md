A water-district board occupies a position of public trust. Directors are entrusted with governmental authority, public money, public infrastructure, and decisions that can materially affect the property, finances, health, safety, and quality of life of the people living within the district. The board's responsibilities therefore extend beyond merely attending meetings and voting on items placed before it by consultants.

### 1. The legal duty: obey the law
The first obligation of a board is simple: **The board must act within the law.** 
>
A board cannot lawfully do something merely because:<br>
- A consultant recommends it.<br>
- The attorney says it is permissible.<br>
- Another district does it.<br>
- It has been done that way for years.<br>
- Nobody previously objected.<br>
- It appears convenient.<br>
- It seems financially beneficial.

The board must have legal authority for its actions and must comply with applicable statutory procedures. Those requirements may arise from the [Texas Constitution](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=CN&chapter=), [Water Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=), [Government Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=), [Local Government Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=LG&chapter=), [Tax Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=TX&chapter=), [Election Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=EL&chapter=), environmental laws, procurement requirements, bond documents, district-specific legislation, and other applicable law.

### 2. The duty to exercise independent judgment
A board is entitled to rely on professionals. It could because water districts could involve technical matters. But:  
>
Hiring a professional / consultant does not mean surrendering governmental judgment. <br>
- Directors should ask questions. <br>
- They should understand significant expenditures. <br>
- They should examine contracts. <br>
- They should question unusual transactions.<br>
- They should understand the financial reports presented to them.<br>
- They should know what an audit says—and what it does not say.<br>
- They should understand major engineering recommendations.<br>
- They should know what legal authority supports significant actions.<br>
- And when something does not make sense, directors have a responsibility to investigate rather than simply approve.

### 3. The duty of stewardship
Public money is held for a public purpose. The board therefore has a stewardship obligation. That means protecting district assets and ensuring that expenditures are:

- Authorized.
- Legitimate.
- Properly documented.
- Reasonably related to district purposes.
- Accurately recorded.
- Properly accounted for.
- Made in accordance with applicable law and district policy.

The board should also insist on reliable financial information. Texas Water Code [Chapter 49](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.49&artSec=) contains financial reporting and audit requirements for districts, including annual audit requirements subject to statutory exceptions.

### 4. The duty to identify conflicts
Water-district directors are subject to the conflict-of-interest provisions of [Chapter 171](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=LG&chapter=LG.171&artSec=) of the Local Government Code. Water Code § [49.058](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.49&artSec=49.058) expressly subjects district directors to that chapter.

[Chapter 171](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=LG&chapter=LG.171&artSec=) requires disclosure and, in circumstances specified by the statute, abstention from participation in matters involving a substantial interest when the statutory conditions are met. This is not a matter of personal preference. Conflict-of-interest rules exist because governmental decisions must not be distorted by undisclosed private economic interests.

### 5. The duty of transparency
A board governs a public entity. Its meetings and records therefore operate within Texas's open-government framework. The board must comply with the [Open Meetings Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=). It must comply with the [Public Information Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.552&artSec=). It must maintain the records required by law. It must not use private communications, informal gatherings, consultants, or procedural devices as a means of defeating transparency requirements.

### 6. The duty to supervise consultants
Perhaps one of the most important principles for residents to understand is this: **Consultants work for the district. The board governs the district.** The board cannot reasonably say:
> 
“Our attorney handled it.” <br>
or: “Our engineer told us it was fine.” <br>
or: “The operator took care of it.” <br>
or: “The bookkeeper prepared the numbers.”

The board is responsible for the governmental decisions it makes. If consultants perform functions that have been delegated to them, the board should nevertheless exercise appropriate oversight. That includes reviewing performance, contracts, invoices, conflicts, deliverables, compliance, and expenditures.

### 7. The ethical duty
The law establishes minimum requirements. Ethics asks a larger question: **What should a public official do when the law does not expressly answer every question?** Something can be technically defensible yet still be ethically questionable. Public confidence depends not merely on avoiding criminal conduct, but on avoiding conduct that reasonably creates the appearance that public office is being used for private advantage. A responsible director should favor:

- Honesty.
- Transparency.
- Impartiality.
- Independence.
- Careful stewardship.
- Disclosure of relevant interests.
- Accurate information.
- Respect for residents.
- Avoidance of favoritism.
- Avoidance of self-dealing.
- Accountability.

### 8. The moral duty
A water district's decisions can affect families for decades. Rates affect household budgets. Taxes affect property owners. Debt can remain outstanding for many years. Infrastructure decisions can affect public safety and property values. Wasteful expenditures ultimately come from somewhere. A director therefore has a moral responsibility to remember that the money being managed represents the contributions of real people.

### 9. The civic duty
Directors are part of the democratic structure of local government. They should welcome informed questions rather than treat questions as hostility. They should recognize that criticism is part of public life. The [Open Meetings Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=) expressly protects public criticism of governmental bodies, subject to other applicable law. The proper response to a difficult question is therefore generally not to suppress the question. It is to answer it with facts and documentation.

### 10. The ultimate test
A useful standard for evaluating a board's conduct is: **Would the board be comfortable explaining the decision, the supporting documents, the expenditure, the relationship involved, and the reasoning behind the decision to every resident whose money helped fund it?** If the answer is yes, transparency should make that explanation relatively easy. If the answer is no, the underlying decision deserves closer examination.
> 
A water-district board is not merely a group of volunteers filling seats at monthly meetings. <br>
- It is the governing body of a governmental entity. <br>
- Its members have legal responsibilities. <br>
- They have ethical responsibilities. <br>
- They have moral responsibilities. <br>
- And they have a civic responsibility to protect the public trust placed in them.

> 📌
> The central principle is simple: **Public office is a responsibility entrusted by the public—not a private privilege.**
