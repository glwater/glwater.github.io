A **Municipal Utility District**, commonly called a **MUD**, is not a private corporation, homeowners' association, or neighborhood association. It is a **governmental entity—a political subdivision of the State of Texas—created to provide public utility and related services to a defined geographic area.**

MUDs are creatures of Texas law. Their authority comes principally from the Texas Constitution and the Texas Water Code, including [Chapter 54](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.54&artSec=), together with the general provisions applicable to all water districts in [Chapter 49](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.49&artSec=).

That distinction matters enormously—when residents pay a MUD for water, wastewater, drainage, taxes, assessments, or other authorized services, they are dealing with a governmental entity exercising powers granted by the State of Texas. The money handled by the district is therefore not simply the money of a private company. The district's board is administering public resources for a defined public purpose.

### What does a MUD do?
Depending on the particular district and its governing documents, a MUD may provide or finance facilities and services such as:

- Water supply and distribution.
- Wastewater collection and treatment.
- Drainage and stormwater facilities.
- Roads and related infrastructure where authorized.
- Parks and recreational facilities where authorized by law.
- Acquisition, construction, operation, and maintenance of utility infrastructure.
- Issuance and repayment of bonds and other obligations.
- Collection of taxes, rates, fees, and assessments authorized by law.

The precise powers of an individual MUD must always be determined by examining the laws applicable to that particular district, including any special legislation, election orders, confirmation documents, bond orders, and other governing instruments.

### Who governs a MUD?
A MUD is governed by a **board of directors**. The board is not merely an advisory committee. It is the governing body responsible for the affairs of the district.

Texas law (Water Code §§ [49.057(a)/(d)](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.49&artSec=49.057) and [49.067(b)](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.49&artSec=49.067)) permits a district to employ or contract with professionals and other service providers to assist in operating the district. Those professionals can include attorneys, engineers, operators, accountants, auditors, financial advisors, tax professionals, and others. But hiring professionals does **not** transfer the board's governmental responsibility to those professionals. A consultant advises, performs, or manages the duties assigned by contract and law. The board remains the governmental body responsible for exercising the district's powers and making the decisions that belong to the board.

### A MUD is a government
This is perhaps the most important point for residents to understand. A MUD is subject to Texas laws governing governmental entities, such as those listed below. The board therefore cannot treat the district as though it were a private business whose affairs belong only to its directors and consultants.

- Specific provisions of the [Water Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=) governing the water district.
- [Government Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=).
- [Open Meetings Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=).
- [Public Information Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.552&artSec=).
- [Election Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=EL&chapter=).
- [Tax Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=TX&chapter=).
- [Local Government Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=LG&chapter=).
- Applicable conflict-of-interest laws.
- Applicable financial reporting and auditing requirements.
- Applicable procurement and contracting requirements.
- Environmental and utility regulations monitored and enforced by the [Texas Commission on Environmental Quality](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/) and the [Public Utility Commission of Texas](https://www.puc.texas.gov/).

### Public money requires public accountability
The public understandably expects a governmental utility district to maintain accurate financial records, properly authorize expenditures, comply with applicable laws, preserve records, disclose public information, conduct its meetings lawfully, and exercise responsible stewardship over the money entrusted to it.

A district may hire private companies (attorney, operator, engineer, bookkeeper, auditor, financial advisor, tax collector, delinquent-tax attorney, trash contractor, website provider, and numerous other vendors) to perform many of its functions. That, however, does **not** make the district itself private and does **not** create a private government. It creates a **government that relies heavily on contractors—and therefore requires an especially attentive governing board.** That is, the widespread use of private consultants to serve a water district makes board oversight especially important.

### Why this matters to residents
Residents often encounter a MUD only when they receive a water bill, see a tax assessment, experience a utility problem, or receive a notice concerning a board meeting. But behind those ordinary interactions is a governmental organization handling substantial public resources. Understanding what a MUD is therefore provides the starting point for understanding a much larger question: **How is the public's business actually being conducted?**

That question leads directly to the Texas [Open Meetings Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.551&artSec=), the Texas [Public Information Act](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=GV&chapter=GV.552&artSec=), financial accountability, conflicts of interest, board responsibilities, and the responsibilities of the professionals hired to serve the district.

> A MUD is a government. Its records are governmental records to the extent provided by law. Its meetings are governmental meetings. Its decisions are governmental decisions. And its directors and consultants operate within a legal framework designed to protect the public whose money and interests the district exists to serve.
