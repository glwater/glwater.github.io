Water districts frequently depend on private professionals and firms to perform work that is essential to government. 

> A district may retain: an attorney, an operator, an engineer, a bookkeeper, an auditor, a financial advisor, a tax collector, 
a delinquent-tax attorney, a trash / solid-waste contractor, a website / technology provider, and other consultants and vendors.

These professionals may provide indispensable expertise. But their private status does **not** mean that their work is outside the public interest. When a private professional is retained by a governmental district, the professional enters a relationship in which professional obligations, contractual obligations, statutory requirements, and public-trust considerations may all apply. The precise duties depend on the person's profession, contract, role, licensing requirements, and the particular circumstances.

### A crucial distinction: consultants are not the board
A consultant does not become the governing board. The board possesses the governmental authority. The consultant generally provides professional advice, services, or operational assistance within the authority delegated by the district. This distinction matters because a consultant should not exercise governmental authority beyond what the law and the district have authorized. Nor should a board use a consultant as a substitute for the board's own legal and governmental responsibilities.

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px; margin-top:-16px;">
<summary><strong>Counsel for the district</strong></summary>
The district counsel's role is especially important. The attorney's fundamental professional responsibility is to provide competent and candid legal advice concerning the district's legal rights, duties, authority, and risks. It is not to generate work that benefits and sustains its own private business. An attorney representing a governmental district should not simply tell the board what it wants to hear. The attorney should identify:

- The applicable law.
- Relevant limitations.
- Material risks.
- Conflicts.
- Required procedures.
- Reasonable alternatives.

When the law is uncertain, the board should be told that it is uncertain. When an action is clearly prohibited, the board should be told so. When a proposed action requires a particular procedure, that procedure should be explained and followed. Legal advice should protect the district—not merely facilitate whatever decision a client may prefer.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The operator</strong></summary>
The operator is responsible for the operational work assigned under the district's contract and applicable regulatory requirements. That can include matters involving:

- Water systems.
- Wastewater systems.
- Treatment facilities.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Maintenance.
- Reporting.
- Emergency response.
- Inspections.
- Records.
- Operational performance.

An operator's work should be accurate, competent, properly documented, and consistent with applicable law and the district's contract. Operational records can also be important public records when they fall within the Public Information Act.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The engineer</strong></summary>
The engineer occupies a position requiring professional competence, technical accuracy, independence, and adherence to applicable engineering requirements. An engineer should not be treated as merely a mechanism for validating a predetermined expenditure. The public should be able to expect that professional engineering judgment is independent and technically grounded.

Engineering recommendations can involve enormous public expenditures. Consequently, an engineer should provide the board with technically defensible information concerning:

- Necessity.
- Scope.
- Cost.
- Alternatives.
- Condition of infrastructure.
- Projected useful life.
- Regulatory requirements.
- Construction requirements.
- Risks.
- Anticipated consequences.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The bookkeeper and accounting personnel</strong></summary>
Bookkeeping is not clerical trivia when millions of dollars of public money are involved. Accounting personnel should maintain accurate records of the following financial activities. Records should be supported by appropriate documentation. Transactions should be classified correctly. Errors should be corrected rather than concealed. The board should be able to rely on financial information when making governmental decisions.

- Receipts.
- Expenditures.
- Accounts payable.
- Accounts receivable.
- Assets.
- Liabilities.
- Bank accounts.
- Restricted funds.
- Debt.
- Taxes.
- Rates.
- Other financial activity applicable to their assignment.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The auditor</strong></summary>
The auditor has a particularly important role. An auditor's independence is fundamental to the credibility of the audit. An audit is not simply a document designed to make the district's finances look official. The auditor is expected to perform an independent professional examination in accordance with the applicable auditing standards and engagement requirements.

The distinction between: **“the books balance”** and **“the financial statements are fairly presented in accordance with the applicable accounting framework”** is important. So is the distinction between an audit and other forms of financial work. An audit does not mean that every transaction is necessarily correct, lawful, or free of fraud. Nor should the absence of an audit finding automatically be represented as proof that no wrongdoing occurred. 

Residents should therefore examine:
- The audit opinion.
- The financial statements.
- Notes to the financial statements.
- Internal-control findings.
- Management letters.
- Significant deficiencies.
- Material weaknesses.
- Prior-year findings.
- Any qualifications or modifications.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The financial advisor</strong></summary>
A financial advisor may assist with debt, bonds, refinancing, financial planning, investment matters, or other financial transactions. The board should understand who is paying the advisor and whether any relationships or compensation arrangements could affect independence. Because financial decisions affect taxpayers and ratepayers for many years, the advisor should provide accurate, understandable, and appropriately disclosed information concerning:

- Costs.
- Interest.
- Fees.
- Alternatives.
- Risks.
- Assumptions.
- The financial consequences of proposed transactions.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The tax collector</strong></summary>

Texas law specifically addresses district tax-collector arrangements. Water Code § [49.059](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=WA&chapter=WA.49&artSec=49.059) permits a district to employ or contract with qualified persons or entities to perform tax-collector functions, subject to statutory requirements. The tax professional therefore occupies a position involving the collection and administration of public revenue. Accuracy, proper accounting, timely deposits, proper records, lawful procedures, and compliance with applicable tax laws are essential.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The delinquent-tax attorney</strong></summary>
Delinquent-tax collection involves the enforcement of governmental claims against taxpayers and property. The attorney handling such matters therefore has responsibilities extending beyond simply generating legal fees. Actions should be:

- Legally authorized.
- Properly documented.
- Accurately calculated.
- Procedurally correct.
- Proportionate to the governing law.
- Consistent with the district's interests.

Because delinquent-tax activity can directly affect residents' property rights, accuracy and procedural compliance are especially important.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The trash contractor</strong></summary>
A trash contractor may appear unrelated to government accountability. It is not. If a district contracts for a service, the district's governing board has a responsibility to ensure that:

- The contract is properly authorized.
- The contractor performs the agreed services.
- Invoices correspond to actual services.
- Payments are properly authorized.
- Contract terms are followed.
- Public funds are not paid for work that was not performed.

The same principle applies to every vendor.
</details>

<details markdown="1" style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
<summary><strong>The website and technology provider</strong></summary>
A website provider may also perform a governmental function. As a website is used to publish governmental information, the accuracy, accessibility, reliability, record preservation, and compliance with applicable legal requirements matter. Technology should facilitate transparency—not become a barrier to it.

- Meeting notices.
- Agendas.
- Minutes.
- Financial information.
- Budgets.
- Contracts.
- Public notices.
- Contact information.
- Other governmental information. 
</details> 

### The common duty of every consultant
Although the legal duties of an attorney differ from those of an engineer, auditor, operator, accountant, or technology provider, several principles apply broadly. A consultant serving a public district should:

**Be competent.** The consultant should possess the professional knowledge and skill necessary to perform the contracted work.

**Be accurate.** Reports, invoices, calculations, recommendations, certifications, and representations should be truthful and accurate.

**Be candid.** Material problems should not be concealed merely because disclosure would be inconvenient.

**Follow the law.** A consultant should not knowingly assist a governmental body in violating applicable law.

**Follow the contract.** The consultant should perform the services actually contracted for and should not charge for services that were not authorized or performed.

**Avoid improper conflicts.** A consultant should identify circumstances that could compromise independence or create divided loyalties.

**Preserve records.** Records relating to governmental work should be maintained and preserved as required by law and contract.

**Respect public accountability.** A consultant should understand that serving a governmental entity is different from serving an ordinary private client.

**Protect public resources.** Public money should be treated with care.

**Speak out when something is wrong.** Perhaps the most important professional principle is this:

**A consultant's responsibility is not to make every decision look acceptable. It is to provide the professional service honestly and competently—even when the truth is inconvenient.**

### The board and the consultants together
There is a natural division of responsibility.
>
Division of responsibility between the board and the consultants:
- **The board governs.**
- **The consultants advise, perform, document, and report within their respective areas of responsibility.**
- Problems can arise when that distinction becomes blurred.
- A board should not blindly follow consultants.
- A consultant should not become the de facto governing authority.
- A professional should not use governmental dependence on expertise as an excuse for inadequate work.
- A board should not use professional advice as a shield against accountability for its own decisions.

The ultimate standard is, and should be, straightforward:

• Every dollar should be traceable.  
• Every significant decision should be authorized.  
• Every material representation should be accurate.  
• Every professional should perform the work for which the district is paying.  
• Every legal requirement should be respected.  
• Every participant should remember that the district exists to serve the public.  
• That is what public trust requires.
